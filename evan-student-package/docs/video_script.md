# MYSSP 2026 summary video — 2.5-minute script and shot list

**Length:** 2:30 (within the 2–3 minute rule)
**Format:** MP4, 1080p, clear audio, no software logos
**Rule:** all three team members appear and speak; no text-heavy slides

| Time | Visual | Speaker | Narration |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:12 | Evan on camera, then a simple animation of a cosmic ray hitting the atmosphere | Evan | “Every minute, about ten thousand cosmic-ray muons pass through every square metre around you. They are free, they are everywhere, and they can cross solid rock.” |
| 0:12–0:30 | Diagram: proton → pion → muon; a clock showing time dilation | Junyi | “A cosmic-ray proton makes pions in the upper atmosphere, and the pions decay into muons. A muon lives only two-point-two microseconds at rest, but it reaches the ground because relativity slows its clock.” |
| 0:30–0:48 | Animation: muon passing through 100 m of limestone; energy-loss curve | Samantha | “A muon loses about two MeV for every gram per square centimetre. One hundred metres of limestone needs about 53 GeV. Only the rare high-energy tail gets through — and that is what makes muography possible.” |
| 0:48–1:05 | Screen recording: the pyramid and detector geometry; the 41 × 41 ray grid | Evan | “We built the whole measurement as a simulation: a 230-metre limestone pyramid, a 10-metre chamber, and a 0.25-square-metre detector looking in 1,681 directions.” |
| 1:05–1:25 | Screen recording: notebook 02 tracing rays; the opacity map appears | Junyi | “For every direction we add up density times length. That is the opacity. The chamber removes about 4,900 grams per square centimetre along the line of sight — close to the 5,000 we calculate by hand.” |
| 1:25–1:45 | Screen recording: expected counts, then the clean ratio map | Samantha | “We divide the chamber target by a twin with the chamber filled solid. The pyramid cancels, and the chamber appears as a 48 per cent excess. This is the reconstructed image.” |
| 1:45–2:05 | Animation: Poisson scatter around the expected curve; exposure curve | Evan | “Poisson noise is the catch. We fix one signal direction and use the expected significance. It grows as root time: three sigma at 4.8 days and five sigma at 13.4 days for the baseline detector.” |
| 2:05–2:22 | Simple graphic: 1,681 pixels; a few random 3-sigma pixels; then the real chamber | Junyi | “A single three-sigma pixel is not enough: in a 1,681-pixel noise-only image, the maximum is already about 3.4 sigma. We quantify that look-elsewhere effect and keep the headline honest.” |
| 2:22–2:35 | Density-systematics graphic; then all three on camera | Samantha | “The biggest limitation is the rock density: a four per cent error can fake a five-sigma signal at long exposure. The result is a benchmark for the model, not a claim about a real pyramid.” |
| 2:35–2:45 | Title card: “13.4 days for 5σ” with the repository URL | All three | “A free cosmic-ray beam, a simple ratio, and careful statistics: that is how we made a mountain transparent.” |

## Production notes

- Record each person in a quiet room at 1080p; use the same framing and lighting.
- Cut between face and screen recording every 10–15 seconds; do not leave a
  static slide on screen for more than 15 seconds.
- Show the notebook run once (ray tracing or the exposure curve) so the audience
  sees that the result is generated, not drawn.
- Do not add background music, heavy transitions, or software watermarks.
- Keep on-screen text to short labels; the narration carries the detail.
- Final check: play the video to someone unfamiliar with the project. They
  should be able to repeat the question, the method, and the 13.4-day result.

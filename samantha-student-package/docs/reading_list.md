# Reading list

Ordered by when to read it, not by importance. Nobody expects you to understand
a research paper completely — read the abstract, look hard at the figures, read
the conclusions, and then go back to whichever paragraph now seems worth it.

## Week 1–2: the essentials

- **`docs/primer.md`** (in this package) — start here. Sections 1–2 in week 1,
  section 3 in week 2.
- **Particle Data Group, "Cosmic Rays" review** — the standard reference for the
  muon flux formula used in the code. Read the first two pages and look at the
  spectrum figure; compare it to the plot you make in notebook 01.

## Week 3–4: the result that made the field famous

- **Morishima, K. et al., "Discovery of a big void in Khufu's Pyramid by
  observation of cosmic-ray muons," *Nature* 552, 386–390 (2017).**
  Read the abstract and study Figures 2 and 3 closely. By week 6 you will be
  producing your own version of Figure 3, and the resemblance is not a
  coincidence.

## Week 5–6: the field in general

- **Bonomi, G. et al., "Applications of cosmic-ray muons," *Progress in Particle
  and Nuclear Physics* 112, 103768 (2020).** A survey of what muography is used
  for: volcanoes, blast furnaces, nuclear reactors, tunnels, archaeology. Skim
  the whole thing, then read whichever section interests you most.
- **Tanaka, H. K. M., et al.** — any of the volcano muography papers. Tanaka's
  group pioneered imaging active volcanoes, and their figures show what the
  method looks like when the target is 1,000 m thick instead of 100 m.

## Week 7–8: for writing your report

- Look again at the Khufu paper, this time for its **structure** rather than its
  physics. Notice how short the abstract is, how much of the argument lives in
  the figures, and how carefully the authors state what they did *not* measure.
  Your report should have the same shape at one-tenth the length.

## If you get interested

The Muon Physics Group at the Tsung-Dao Lee Institute works on muon imaging of
tunnels and geology in Shanghai, alongside precision measurements of the muon's
magnetic moment at Fermilab and its electric dipole moment at PSI in
Switzerland. If any of that sounds interesting, say so — the natural next step
after this project is a longer one, and there is more to do than there are
people to do it.

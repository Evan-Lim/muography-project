# Seeing Through Solid Rock

### Finding a hidden chamber with cosmic-ray muons

**MYSSP Project P-1 · 8 weeks · 8 hours per week**
Mentor: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Khaw Kim Siang, Muon Physics Group, Tsung-Dao Lee Institute,
Shanghai Jiao Tong University

---

Every minute, about ten thousand cosmic-ray muons pass through every square
metre of the ground you are standing on. They come from the deaths of stars, they
arrive at nearly the speed of light, and they are heavy enough to travel through
hundreds of metres of solid rock before stopping.

That last fact makes them useful. Count how many muons emerge from a mountain from many viewing directions, and
you can work out what is inside it — without drilling, without touching it,
without ever going in. In 2017 a team used exactly this
method to find a chamber inside the Great Pyramid of Khufu that had been sealed
for four and a half thousand years.

Over the next eight weeks you are going to build a working simulation of that
measurement, and use it to answer a question with a number:

> **Under this simulation model, how long would a 0.25 m² detector observing the baseline pyramid need before the expected chamber signal reaches 5σ?**

The headline number is an expected sensitivity of the simulated setup, not an
automatic prediction for a real detector. Individual noisy simulations will
fluctuate around it.

## Getting started

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
jupyter notebook
```

Then open `notebooks/00_start_here.ipynb` and work through it. Read
`docs/primer.md` sections 1–2 in your first week.

If `import muography` fails, you probably skipped the first setup cell. Every
notebook adds `code/` to its Python path automatically; run that cell first.
For notebooks 03–05, the student functions come from `code/student_analysis.py`,
so those notebooks can be restarted and run independently.

## What is in here

```
notebooks/     Your workspace. Six notebooks, in order. Each can be restarted independently after you have implemented the shared analysis functions. This is where you work.
code/          muography.py -- supplied physics and geometry.
              student_analysis.py -- the student-owned analysis functions.
              project_config.py -- the single source of baseline parameters.
docs/          primer.md, the weekly plan, the reading list, the report template.
figures/       Reference figures, so you know what you are aiming at.
               `docs/project_sheet.md` is the one-page specification.
logs/          Your weekly updates.
```

`figures/void_emerging.gif` is a mentor-generated reference animation. Use it
only to understand the expected qualitative behaviour; your final figures must
be generated by your own code.

## How the notebooks work

Each contains three kinds of cell: **explanation** (read it), **given code**
(run it), and **`# YOUR TURN`** (write it). The `YOUR TURN` cells are the
project. Where a cell says *check yourself*, there is a number you should be
able to reproduce — if you cannot, that is the single most useful thing to bring
to your weekly meeting.

| Notebook | Weeks | What you build |
|---|---|---|
| `00_start_here` | 1 | Setup and a numpy warm-up |
| `01_muons_and_rock` | 1–2 | The muon spectrum; how much energy it takes to cross rock |
| `02_ray_tracing` | 3–4 | A virtual pyramid, and a map of how much rock lies in every direction |
| `03_first_image` | 5 | Expected muon counts, and dividing out the shape of the pyramid |
| `04_noise_and_exposure` | 5–6 | Random noise, significance, and **the headline result** |
| `05_parameter_study` | 6–7 | What makes a chamber easy or hard to find |

## What you will hand in

- A repository whose notebooks run independently from a fresh kernel, using the shared analysis module
- The before-and-after image pair: the true chamber, and the one you recovered
- The expected-significance exposure curve, and its interpolated first 5σ crossing
- Two parameter studies of your choosing
- A short report (`docs/report_template.tex`) and a demo-day presentation

## Two habits

**Commit every session, including the broken ones.** `git commit -m "opacity
comes out negative above 30 degrees, no idea why"` is a better research record
than a clean repository that appears from nowhere in week 8. Research is mostly
the parts that did not work, and the log of those parts is worth keeping.

**Send your weekly update even in a week where nothing worked.** Especially
then. Copy `docs/weekly_log_template.md`, fill in four short paragraphs, send it
before the meeting. A week spent stuck on one bug is completely normal; a week
spent stuck on one bug *in silence* is the only real way this project can fail.
